How it works

It simulates your house hour by hour from now until the end of the third day, once for each option, and compares the cost.

  • Solar is the Open-Meteo forecast of sunlight on panels at your roof's angle, which already accounts for cloud. It's scaled by panel size and performance.
  • House use follows a typical Australian daily pattern (small morning bump, big evening peak) scaled to your daily total.
  • Each hour: solar powers the house and car first, then fills the battery, then exports. If feed-in is negative and curtailment is on, the extra solar is switched off instead. When solar runs short, the battery covers the house (and the car, if allowed) down to your reserve. The grid covers the rest.
  • Prices are Amber's half-hourly forecast for buying and feed-in. Where Amber's forecast runs out (usually after tomorrow), it uses the typical forecast price for that time of day. One-off spikes aren't copied forward.
  • Battery on: the car charges from plug-in time and the battery supplies it. Battery off: the car charges in the cheapest block before it leaves and the battery is kept for the house. Solar tomorrow: the car charges in the cheapest daytime block tomorrow.
  • Looking three days ahead catches the trap where tomorrow is sunny but the next two days are grey. If the battery is already full tomorrow either way, those later days don't change the answer.
  • Spike protection: an hour is protected if Amber flags it as a possible spike, its forecast is above your spike price, or Amber's high estimate is over double it. The car can't take the battery energy the house needs to get through those hours. That includes the hours in between, and it assumes solar comes in 30% under forecast, to be safe.
  • Charge limit: heat plus a full pack is what wears out NMC batteries. If the car's parked outside, round tomorrow's top temperature to the nearest 10 and take it off 100. So 23°C gives 80% and 34°C gives 70%. It never goes below 50%.
  • It's a forecast. Treat close calls as close calls.
